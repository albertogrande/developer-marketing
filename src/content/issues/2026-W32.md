Between Wednesday and Friday last week, three terminal coding agents launched:
Meta's [Muse Code](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/05/meta-launches-muse-code-an-ai-agent-for-large-code-bases/)
in beta, Memcode in public beta, and Clark Code as Apache-2.0 open source. Read
the three pitches side by side and notice what none of them says: *ours writes
better code*. Meta competes on price, Memcode on persistent repository memory,
Clark on openness. A fourth launch in the same window, Mirafold, doesn't sell an
agent at all — it sells a browser UI over the incumbents, and an accessory
market is the oldest sign there is that a category is commoditizing underneath.
The [pattern item](/signals#2026-08-07-coding-agent-entrants-pattern) has the
receipts.

This is the third week of the same arc. [Two weeks ago](/issues/2026-W30) was
what an AI seat costs. [Last week](/issues/2026-W31) was what an exit window
signals. This week the coding-agent category — the loudest category in
devtools — stopped differentiating on capability entirely. When the features
converge, the marketing surface moves to the terms: what the price buys, what
the data clause takes, what the exit costs, what the channel owes you. If your
own pitch still leads with capability, this is the week to re-read it.

## The discount is a data trade

The sharpest move of the week is Muse Code's pricing table. Standard
pay-as-you-go is $1.25 per million input tokens and $4.25 per million output.
A "contributor" tier prices the same product at
[$0.10 and $0.20](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/08/05/meta-muse-code-for-mac/) —
roughly 12x cheaper on input, 21x on output — in exchange for Meta training on
your prompts and completions. Meta AI chief Alexandr Wang pitched cost as the
sell — "an incredibly good option... especially from a cost perspective," he
told TechCrunch — against incumbents like Claude Code and Codex, but the
structure is the story: this is, as far as we can find, the first
coding-agent vendor to price a discount explicitly as a data trade rather
than as volume or seats.

That makes a training-data policy a *priced* variable for the first time.
Until Wednesday, "we don't train on your code" was a compliance line buried in
a trust center. Meta just published its market rate: about 90–95% off list.
Every developer-facing AI vendor now has a number attached to a question
enterprise procurement was already asking.

The play, if you sell an AI devtool: decide your answer to data-for-discount
*before* a competitor prices it for you, and move your training-data position
out of the FAQ and onto the pricing page, where it now does positioning work.
The [guide's pricing section](/guide/01-positioning-for-developers) has argued
since the Cerebras episode that a published cap is a trust surface; a
published data clause just became one too.

## The gate at the front, the clock at the back

Two smaller stories this week are the same story: the terms of *being on a
platform* are becoming public, comparable numbers.

At the front gate, developer Josh Symonds published a
[dated timeline](/signals#2026-08-04-anthropic-mcp-directory-stranded-integrator)
of his MCP connector's path through Anthropic's directory review: submitted
March 22, told it was "in the review queue" as late as June 15, then told July
30 that the queue had been discarded and he should resubmit — while OpenAI
approved the same connector in 29 days. One account, not a pattern; but it is
the first concrete stranded-integrator story since
[the August 4 piece](/articles/2026-08-04-mcp-directory-review) argued the
directory is a gated distribution channel, and it puts numbers on the gate. If
a platform directory is in your channel mix, its review queue is now a risk
line item: dual-list where you can, and keep an install path you own.

At the back door, GitHub gave Spark users
[27 days' notice](/signals#2026-08-05-github-spark-deprecation) — signups
frozen immediately, export by August 31, and apps built on the `llm()`
function already broken because the service behind it retired July 30. That is
the shortest window yet in the ledger this site keeps (MCP: 12 months; GitHub
Models: six weeks; HCP Vagrant, [announced the same week](/signals#2026-08-04-hashicorp-hcp-vagrant-eol):
~10 months). [Wednesday's dive](/deep-dives/2026-08-06-the-deprecation-that-didnt-burn-anyone)
made the argument in full: the window is the headline, but the mechanics
decide who gets burned. Spark now sits at the bottom of both scales.

## Publish the number that hurts

The content move of the week is Sentry's
[build-in-public post](/signals#2026-08-06-sentry-seer-review-numbers) on
routing its Seer agent's bug-fix PRs to the right engineer through Slack.
The numbers are modest and useful — action rate on those PRs up about 21%,
48-hour response rate up about 13% — but the reason the post travels is the
third number: close-without-merge *rose* 12.5%, and Sentry printed it, then
argued why that's healthy (engineers often chose a broader fix than the
agent's narrow one). Seer costs $40 per active contributor per month, so this
is commercial content; it earns the read anyway because the uncomfortable
number makes the flattering ones legible. That's the
[dogfooding-receipt pattern](/articles/2026-08-01-cloudflare-dogfoods-cdnjs)
again: proof you can check beats a claim you must trust — and a claim that
costs you something reads as proof.

## Things to watch

1. **Someone answers the data trade, or nobody does.** The
   [pattern item's](/signals#2026-08-07-coding-agent-entrants-pattern) call:
   an incumbent — Anthropic, OpenAI, GitHub — ships or publicly rules out a
   data-for-discount tier by end of August. Either answer is informative:
   a match confirms the squeeze; explicit refusal makes "we don't train on
   your code" the counter-positioning at full price. Silence past September
   would say the entrants are noise.
2. **Cerebras' free-tier sunset lands August 17.** The test, set in
   [the deprecation dive](/deep-dives/2026-08-06-the-deprecation-that-didnt-burn-anyone):
   a clean landing returns deprecation-named errors against a stable model
   catalog; a dirty one is silent 404s. Checkable the same day; we log it
   either way next week.
3. **The MCP directory gets a second account, or an SLA.** By end of August:
   either another integrator publishes a dated timeline corroborating the
   Symonds account, or Anthropic publishes review-queue expectations (an SLA,
   an appeals path, a status page). A second account makes it a pattern we
   cover as one; a published SLA closes the story in Anthropic's favor.
