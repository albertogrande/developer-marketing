## What it covers

A solo host episode: Jack Bridger summarizes Adam Frankel's "technical
advisory board" (TAB) framework — a structured, unpaid, one-on-one customer
research program — and adds his own experience running one for his own
voice-AI product, including where he departs from Adam's method.

## Claims worth checking

- [02:16–02:39] TAB structure as defined: one-on-one calls with prospective/
  current customers, 30 minutes, once a month, for six months (~3 hours of
  their time total) — explicitly not a sales process. Framework as stated by
  Frankel, relayed secondhand; Bridger confirms he uses the same cadence.
- [03:31–04:53] The core interview question — "if you could wave a magic
  wand and do anything you can't do today, what would it be?" — is credited
  to Cindy Alvarez (author, *Lean Customer Development*), with the explicit
  warning not to lead the interviewee: "it's amazing how easily they'll be
  led" once seeded with a suggested answer.
- [06:40–07:52] A second question probes urgency/timing: "what's different
  about the world now such that this is more valuable than it would have
  been [before]?" Bridger notes he shortens the reference window to
  "a year ago" or "six months ago" rather than Frankel's five-to-ten years,
  because AI-adjacent problem spaces move faster — his own adaptation, not
  Frankel's original guidance.
- [08:34–09:11] Explicit rule: never run TAB interviews as group calls — the
  faster/more dominant person answers and others disengage; one-on-one only.
- [09:44–10:19] Compensation is deliberately not equity or money — swag and
  gratitude only; the filter is that a good TAB member cares enough about
  the problem to want to talk about it.
- [11:53–12:15] Recruiting ratio stated: expect to need ~50 outreach
  messages (personalized, praising something specific about the person) for
  every 1 person who joins the board — 250 messages to fill a 10-person TAB.
  Presented as Adam's rule of thumb, not a measured conversion rate.
- [12:49–15:39] Post-interview analysis uses Alexander Osterwalder's Value
  Proposition Design (pains / gains / jobs-to-be-done) per persona; Bridger
  says it commonly collapses 10–12 theorized personas down to as few as 3
  after real interviews, and recommends a startup focus on one persona at a
  time rather than Frankel's fuller multi-persona version — an explicit
  simplification he flags Frankel might disagree with.

## Why it matters here

A fully operational, named framework for structured customer-discovery
interviews that shapes both product and positioning — relevant to
`01-positioning-for-developers` and the thin `03-devrel-and-community`
section (the champions-program-ledger evergreen candidate is adjacent: both
are unpaid, swag-compensated, structured advisor relationships). Weakest
part: the core framework is relayed secondhand from Frankel's book/blog
post rather than stated directly by him in this episode, and the specific
numbers (the 50:1 outreach ratio, the "nothing valuable after the first
thirty minutes" claim) are his rules of thumb, not measured outcomes — cite
the practice as Frankel's attributed method, not as a verified result.
